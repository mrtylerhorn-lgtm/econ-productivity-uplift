        Rules and how this could be wrong
        • Productivity is noisy and revised. Early estimates move; three years is a short window. The noise band in chart 1 treats quarters as independent, which is rough.
        • Growth accounting relies on the SF Fed's method (Basu, Fernald and Kimball; Fernald 2014), which infers how hard people and machines are worked from hours per worker. Its output measure averages the product and income sides of GDP, so its labor-productivity figure differs a little from the BLS headline in chart 1.
        • The industry chart has few dots. Adoption is a recent worker survey, matched to industries by name; output by industry is revised heavily; hours for finance and for professional, scientific and technical services are estimated from employment times their parent sector's weekly hours. Information is drawn apart because it builds and sells AI. Farms, mining, utilities, real estate and government are left out because their output swings with commodity prices, weather or imputed rents.
        • The imported share is rough. Imports don't reduce GDP; they are subtracted only because purchases of foreign-made goods are already counted in spending. Chart 4 nets all computer imports against business investment, but some imported computers go to households and government, so the U.S.-made part is understated somewhat. Data centers' buildings and power equipment are not included, because BEA does not publish them separately each quarter.
        • Job and income signals have other causes. Tech hiring slumped in 2023 as interest rates rose. Young workers' unemployment rises in every slowdown. The labor share is pay per hour divided by output per hour, so a fast productivity quarter pulls it down until pay catches up.
        • GDP may miss what AI produces, such as free services and quality gains (others argue the gap is small; Syverson 2017), and firms adopting a general-purpose technology often see productivity dip before it rises (the J-curve; Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson).
        • Not yet tracked: employment by AI exposure within occupations (needs Census microdata), Census business AI adoption, data-center construction, and industry concentration.
