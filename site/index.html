      How the verdict is decided
      What theory says to look for

      Working smarter vs harder (Basu, Fernald and Kimball; Klein 2026). Output per hour rises when people work longer and harder or use more machines, not only when they get more efficient. Only the efficiency part lasts.

      The J-curve (Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson). Firms adopting a general-purpose technology first spend on training and reorganizing, which holds measured productivity down before it rises. Heavy spending with little measured gain fits this story, at least for a while.

      Competence vs coordination (Imas, Koh and Legg, 2026). Smarter AI agents shift wages and the mix of people and machines gradually, while firms stay small. Big jumps in industry concentration, jobs and pay come only once agents coordinate well in large teams. Workers whose skills AI matches see their pay pulled toward the cost of computing, while people who direct and coordinate work gain. Their key claim for a tracker like this: wages and job roles move first, so flat productivity or employment totals are weak evidence that nothing is happening.

      Late-1990s precedent. Output per hour picked up around 1997, years after computers and the internet were widely hyped and priced into tech stocks.

      Does the start date matter?

      Average yearly growth in output per hour from different starting points to the latest quarter. The pickup does not depend on picking the end of 2023.

      Measurement cross-checks

      Different ways of measuring output and efficiency, since the end of 2023 against 2004–19. If the pickup came from a quirk in one series, the others would disagree.

      The nonfinancial corporate measure covers employees of corporations outside finance. It leaves out the self-employed, government, and owner-occupied housing, whose output is hard to measure. Real GDI and GDP should match in principle; when income grows faster than output, the official output figures may be too low.

      Growth accounting: the latest SF Fed quarters

      Annualized quarterly growth rates, percent. Labor productivity here is the SF Fed's business-output measure, which averages the product and income sides of GDP, so it differs from the BLS headline. Inputs per hour is labor productivity minus TFP: the contribution of more capital per hour and a more skilled workforce. Utilization-adjusted TFP ("underlying efficiency") equals TFP minus utilization.

      How the utilization adjustment works, and why it might mislead

      The SF Fed method (Basu, Fernald and Kimball; Fernald 2014) uses hours per worker as a stand-in for unseen effort and for how long machines run. Industries are weighted by how sensitive they are to the business cycle, not by headcount. Klein, using John Fernald's industry data, found that almost all of the recent utilization signal comes from manufacturing, and that the fabricated-metals workweek tracks it most closely.

      Can factory overtime tell us anything about software that writes code or folds proteins? Klein's first guess was no. Still, the method has a strong record through past cycles, including the pandemic. The industry data in Readout 3 are a separate check: professional and business services sped up more than hands-on industries, though manufacturing sped up too.

      Timing matters too. In the 1990s, output per hour did not accelerate until 1997, years after tech stocks were already priced for a boom.

      How this tracker could be wrong
      • The industry test is rough. Information is kept out of the counted test because it includes data centers, software publishers and telecom, which make and sell computing; capital spending and layoffs there can raise output per hour without anyone else getting more efficient. The "AI users" proxy, professional and business services, also includes office support, cleaning and staffing; the narrower professional, scientific and technical services industry has no matching hours series on FRED. Finance and insurance, a heavy AI user, is missing for the same reason. Education, often rated highly exposed to AI, is grouped with health care, so it is left out of the test.
      • Industry data is rough. BEA's quarterly industry output is heavily revised, and payroll hours leave out the self-employed, so the industry figures can differ from the national ones.
      • Ten quarters is short. Productivity growth is noisy, and early estimates are revised. Readout 2 shows how often chance alone produced similar stretches.
      • GDP may miss what AI produces. Free services, quality gains and in-house software are hard to price, which would make measured productivity too low. Others argue the measurement gap is too small to matter (Syverson 2017).
      • The J-curve. Firms adopting a new general-purpose technology first spend on training and reorganizing, which can hold measured productivity down before it rises (Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson).
      • Other explanations. The post-pandemic shake-up of jobs and firms, a shift in who is working, and more capital per worker can all raise output per hour without AI.
      • The work signals have other causes. Tech hiring slumped in 2023 as interest rates rose and firms trimmed pandemic-era hiring, before most firms used AI. A 2022 tax change made software R&D more expensive to deduct until 2025. Graduates' edge over other young workers swings with the business cycle and was already small at the end of 2023. Median pay by occupation shifts as the mix of workers changes.
      • Survey limits. The AI-use survey is young (since mid-2024) and noisy. The occupation pay and graduate unemployment figures come from the household survey, which skipped October 2025.
      • Not yet tracked. Industry concentration, the clearest sign of the large-firm shift Imas, Koh and Legg describe, isn't published quickly enough to track weekly. Census business AI adoption (BTOS) and data-center construction aren't published in a form this page can download automatically, and neither is CBO's January 2020 productivity forecast.
