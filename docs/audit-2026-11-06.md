# Audit, Nov 6, 2026: what the site should answer

## The big-picture question
"Is AI changing the U.S. economy yet, how far along is it, and where would we see it first?" The site answered a narrower question: "Has measured productivity broken out, by Klein's tests?" That is one stage of the story, and usually a late one.

## Problems with v5
1. **Too anchored on one framework.** The verdict rested on Klein's framing (end-2023 start, SF Fed utilization, 1990s real rates). The utilization-adjusted measure is informative but contested. The rates readout is about monetary policy implications, not detection.
2. **Productivity only.** Imas, Koh and Legg (DeepMind, Oct 2026) argue that wages and job roles move first, while GDP, productivity and industry concentration move later. On that view, "productivity hasn't broken out" says little about whether AI is affecting the economy. The site tracked no wages, jobs, roles or adoption.
3. **It couldn't answer the claims people actually make.** Brynjolfsson (Nov 2026): "3.6% in Q3, more than double the 1.5% 2007–19 average; ahead of CBO's pre-pandemic forecast; winning my bet with Gordon." The site had no CBO benchmark, no bet tracker, and no single-quarter-in-context check.
4. **No adoption measure.** The St. Louis Fed's Real-Time Population Survey (on FRED) tracks the share of workers using generative AI, overall and by occupation and industry.
5. **No "who gains" measure.** If AI substitutes for labor, labor's share of income falls even as productivity rises. That is central to the wage question.

## What a Fed researcher would add (all on FRED, fetched weekly by the GitHub Action)
- **Adoption:** generative AI use at work (RPS), overall and for exposed vs hands-on occupations.
- **Money:** real tech investment (already tracked) and tech investment relative to payroll (Imas et al. explicitly call for tracking compute against payroll).
- **Work, the earliest signal:**
  - Unemployment of new college graduates (20–24) vs older graduates. This is the "entry-level roles closest to benchmark tasks" signal.
  - Jobs in exposed services: computer systems design, office and administrative support occupations.
  - Pay: management vs office-support median weekly earnings. Imas et al. predict coordinators gain while routine specialists' pay converges to compute cost.
- **Output:** the existing productivity tests, plus a CBO January 2020 forecast benchmark, the Brynjolfsson–Gordon bet tracker, and the latest quarter put in context.
- **Who gains:** labor share and real compensation vs productivity.

## Design
- **Structure:** a stage strip (Adoption → Money → Work → Output → Who gains), each computed from fixed rules.
- **Headline:** keep "Is AI showing up in productivity?" as a sub-verdict, but lead with where the economy sits in the sequence.
- **Klein:** keep his useful insight (separating working smarter from working harder) as one test among several. Demote the 1990s real-rates band to context.

## Second audit, Nov 6 afternoon: is the evidence right?
An independent review (fresh agent, recomputing from raw data) plus my own check found these problems, all fixed:
1. **Post-hoc test.** The cross-industry slope (chart 3) became the counted industry test after adding two industries made it significant. The same regression on 2019–23, before AI use at work, already showed a slope of +0.6 (chance 10%); against each industry's own 2019–23 pace it is +0.3 (24%). Reverted: the pre-specified gap test counts again; the slope is shown as exploratory.
2. **Headline misattribution.** The top headline explained Q3's 3.6% with SF Fed growth accounting that ends in Q2. Rewritten.
3. **Capital deepening mislabeled.** It is capital per hour (all capital), a lasting gain, and can include AI. Split out IT capital from the SF Fed's capital-by-type sheet: 0.57 since 2023 vs 0.46 in 2004–19 and 0.81 in 1996–2003. Most of the rise is other capital.
4. **Efficiency overstated as flat.** 90% range since 2023 is −1.3 to +1.5%/yr. Now shown.
5. **Import netting unreliable.** Netting all computer imports (incl. households, government, tariff stockpiling) against business investment implied ~zero U.S.-made equipment. Chart 4 now shows gross tech investment, with imports as a caveat. Corrected the 1990s peak (best four quarters 0.9, to 1999Q3).
6. **Occupation chart straddled the 2020 CPS recoding.** Now each group vs all groups over the same period: slope ~0 (r 0.04). The earlier "suggestive" result was an artifact.
7. **Start date.** Graduates' gap with all workers was already above its 2000–19 max by Dec 2022; work tests rerun from Dec 2022 give the same tiers.
8. **Bugs.** SF Fed summary rows overwrote three quarters (regex); hard-coded "more than double"; 1990s boom dated inconsistently (now 1996–2003 throughout; path comparison 7.6%, not 9.1%).
9. **Disclosures.** Multiple one-sided tests (one "clear" by chance is plausible), overlapping windows, revisions, hedonic prices, utilization vs rising unemployment.
